Graph Neural Networks
for On-Chain Address Profiling

Why looking at one address at a time is like trying to understand a city by studying a single building. FidesOrigin's GNN engine sees the whole graph.

In January 2026, a wallet address appeared on our monitoring system with a risk score of 34 — well below the 80-point quarantine threshold. On paper, it looked fine. The address had no direct sanctions hits, a normal transaction history, and no obvious red flags. A traditional rules-based system would have cleared it.

But our Graph Neural Network (GNN) engine flagged it anyway. The reason? This address was two hops away from a known mixing service, connected through a chain of four intermediate wallets that had each been created within the same 24-hour window and funded from the same source. The GNN didn't just see the address — it saw its neighborhood. And the neighborhood was guilty by association.

Three days later, that address appeared on the OFAC SDN list. Our traditional rules engine would have missed it entirely until the official update. The GNN caught it early.

This is the power of relationship-based risk analysis. And it's why single-address profiling is fundamentally inadequate for on-chain compliance.

The Limits of Single-Address Analysis

Traditional chain analysis — the kind that powers most compliance APIs today — works like this: extract features from an address (balance, transaction count, age, known associations), feed them into a classifier, get a risk score. It's essentially a tabular machine learning problem. Each address is a row in a spreadsheet. The model never sees the connections between rows.

This approach has three fatal weaknesses on a blockchain:

1. Adversaries exploit graph structure deliberately. A sophisticated attacker doesn't use one wallet. They use fifty. They peel funds through intermediate addresses. They use privacy pools, cross-chain bridges, and DeFi protocols as laundering layers. Each individual address looks innocuous. The pattern across addresses is the signal. A tabular model is blind to this.

2. New addresses have no history. A freshly-created wallet has zero transactions, zero balance, and zero features. A tabular model has nothing to work with. But a GNN can infer risk from its connections: who funded it? Who does it transact with? What subgraph does it belong to? The graph reveals what the transaction history conceals.

3. Risk is contextual, not intrinsic. An address that receives funds from a sanctioned entity is risky. The same address receiving funds from a legitimate exchange is not. Risk is a property of relationships, not just the address itself. You cannot compute it without the graph.

Chainalysis and Elliptic have built enormous databases of labeled addresses. Their models are sophisticated. But they're still fundamentally tabular: "Address X has property Y, therefore risk Z." The graph is an afterthought — a visualization tool, not a computational model.

We flipped this. In FidesOrigin, the graph is the model.

The On-Chain Graph: Nodes, Edges, and Features

To understand how GNNs work for address profiling, let's define the graph we're operating on.

Nodes are wallet addresses and smart contracts. Each node has a feature vector that captures its intrinsic properties: transaction count, average transaction value, time since first transaction, balance history variance, contract interaction diversity, and so on. We extract ~40 features per node from on-chain data.

Edges represent transactions. An edge from address A to address B means A sent funds to B. Edges are weighted by transaction value, frequency, and temporal proximity. Multiple transactions between the same pair are aggregated into a single edge with composite weights. We currently track ~2.3 billion edges across Ethereum, Base, and Polygon.

Node labels come from multiple sources: OFAC sanctions lists, exchange hot/cold wallet designations, known DeFi protocol contracts, phishing scam databases, and our own manual investigations. Only a tiny fraction of nodes are labeled — typically less than 0.01%. The challenge is propagating these labels through the graph to unlabeled nodes.

The graph scale: Our production graph currently contains ~180 million nodes and ~2.3 billion edges, updated in real time from Ethereum mainnet, Base, and Polygon. The full graph is too large for a single GPU, so we use subgraph sampling and mini-batch training.

From Graph to Risk Score: How GNNs Work

A Graph Neural Network operates on a simple but powerful idea: a node's representation should be a function of its own features and the representations of its neighbors. Information propagates through the graph, one hop at a time, with each layer of the network allowing the model to see farther into the neighborhood.

We use a GraphSAGE (Graph Sample and Aggregated) architecture with an attention mechanism inspired by GAT (Graph Attention Networks). Here's why this combination works for our use case:

GraphSAGE is designed for inductive learning — it can generate embeddings for nodes it has never seen during training. This is critical for on-chain compliance, where new addresses are created every second. We can't retrain the model every time a new wallet appears. GraphSAGE's sampling approach lets us compute embeddings for new nodes on the fly, using only their local neighborhood.

The attention mechanism (from GAT) lets the model learn which neighbors matter most. Not all connections are equal. A $10 transfer from a random address is noise. A $500,000 transfer from a known exchange is signal. The attention weights learn this automatically from the data, without hand-crafted rules.

The forward pass works like this. For each node, we sample a fixed number of neighbors (we use k=2 hops with 25 neighbors per hop). We aggregate the neighbor features using a learned aggregation function — in our case, a learned weighted mean where the weights come from the attention mechanism. We then concatenate the aggregated neighbor representation with the node's own features and pass it through a non-linear transformation. Repeat for L layers (we use L=3), and you have a node embedding that encodes information from the L-hop neighborhood.

GNN Forward Pass (Simplified) Python / PyTorch Geometric 
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch_geometric.nn import SAGEConv, GATConv

class RiskGNN(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels, hidden_channels, out_channels):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv1 = SAGEConv(in_channels, hidden_channels)
        self.conv2 = GATConv(hidden_channels, hidden_channels, heads=4, concat=False)
        self.conv3 = SAGEConv(hidden_channels, out_channels)
        self.classifier = torch.nn.Linear(out_channels, 3)  # ALLOW, WARN, BLOCK

    def forward(self, x, edge_index, edge_attr):
        # Layer 1: SAGE aggregation, 1-hop neighbors
        x = self.conv1(x, edge_index)
        x = F.relu(x)
        x = F.dropout(x, p=0.3, training=self.training)

        # Layer 2: GAT with attention, 2-hop neighbors
        x = self.conv2(x, edge_index, edge_attr)
        x = F.elu(x)
        x = F.dropout(x, p=0.3, training=self.training)

        # Layer 3: SAGE aggregation, 3-hop neighbors
        x = self.conv3(x, edge_index)

        # Risk classification
        return self.classifier(x)

The output is a 3-way classification (ALLOW, WARN, BLOCK) plus a continuous risk score from 0 to 100. The risk score is derived from the logits using a calibrated softmax — we use temperature scaling on a held-out validation set to ensure the scores are probabilistically meaningful.

Three GNN-Powered Detection Patterns

The GNN doesn't just produce a risk score. It enables specific detection capabilities that are impossible with tabular methods.

1. Community Discovery: Mapping Mixing Networks

Mixing services and tumblers are the Achilles' heel of on-chain compliance. They deliberately obfuscate the flow of funds by aggregating inputs from many sources and redistributing them to many outputs. From a single-address perspective, a mixer output address looks like it received funds from a "legitimate" service. From a graph perspective, it's a node in a densely-connected cluster with specific topological signatures.

Our GNN learns to recognize these signatures: unusually high in-degree and out-degree, short average path lengths to other mixer-related addresses, temporal clustering (many related transactions within a narrow time window), and value homogeneity (similar amounts in and out). The model doesn't need to know the name of the mixer. It learns the pattern from the graph structure.

In production, this has allowed us to identify 14 previously-unknown mixing services that were not in any public database. One of them had processed over $40M in volume before our detection.

2. Anomaly Detection: Spotting Sybil Attacks

A Sybil attack in the on-chain context involves creating many pseudonymous identities (wallets) to game a protocol — for airdrop farming, governance manipulation, or wash trading. Each individual wallet looks normal. But the GNN sees the creation pattern: dozens of wallets funded from the same source, with similar transaction patterns, never interacting with each other (to avoid obvious clustering), but all connected through common DeFi protocols within narrow time windows.

The GNN computes embeddings for all nodes. We then run DBSCAN clustering on these embeddings. Normal users form diffuse, low-density clusters. Sybil networks form tight, high-density clusters with distinct separation from legitimate users. The clustering is unsupervised — we don't need labeled Sybil data. The graph structure is the signal.

We detected a Sybil farm of 3,200 wallets targeting an airdrop campaign in March 2026. The farm had been designed to evade simple heuristics (each wallet had "unique" funding sources via a peeling chain). The GNN caught the common structural signature in the embedding space.

3. Risk Propagation: How Contagion Spreads

Perhaps the most powerful GNN capability is risk propagation. When a new address is added to the OFAC SDN list, the GNN doesn't just flag that address. It recomputes risk scores for every node within its 3-hop neighborhood. Addresses that were previously low-risk may become elevated-risk because they're now connected — directly or indirectly — to a sanctioned entity.

This is not simple transitive closure. The GNN learns how risk propagates: a $10M transfer from a sanctioned address is high risk. A $50 transfer three hops away, through an intermediate exchange, is lower risk. The attention weights in the GAT layer learn these nuanced propagation rules from the training data.

When the OFAC updated its sanctions list in April 2026, our system recomputed risk scores for 2.1 million addresses within 5 minutes. Of these, 847 addresses crossed the quarantine threshold and were automatically flagged in the Risk Registry. A rules-based system would have caught only the directly-listed addresses — 23 of them.

The Real-Time Feature Engineering Pipeline

A model is only as good as the data pipeline that feeds it. Our pipeline operates in a continuous loop:

Data Ingestion: We subscribe to WebSocket feeds from multiple RPC providers for Ethereum, Base, and Polygon. New transactions are buffered in Kafka, partitioned by block number. We process ~15,000 transactions per second at peak.

Graph Construction: Every new transaction updates the graph in near-real time. We maintain the graph in a distributed key-value store (ScyllaDB) with adjacency lists for each node. Edge weights are computed on insertion: value normalized by sender's historical average, time-decay factor, and token type (stablecoins weighted higher than memecoins for risk purposes).

GNN Inference: Every 5 minutes, we trigger a batched inference job. We identify all nodes that have changed since the last run (new transactions, updated labels, or manual annotations) and their 3-hop neighborhoods. We compute embeddings for this subgraph using our production GNN model running on a GPU cluster. Average inference time: ~90 seconds for a batch of 500,000 nodes.

Label Generation: The GNN outputs risk scores and classifications. We apply post-processing rules: addresses with BLOCK classification and confidence > 0.95 are flagged for immediate action. WARN classifications with confidence > 0.8 are queued for human review. Everything else is stored in the risk database with its score.

Oracle Push: The updated risk scores are formatted into a Merkle tree and pushed to the on-chain Oracle contract. The Oracle then updates the RiskRegistry, making the new scores available to the Guard and Compliance Engine. Total latency from transaction ingestion to on-chain availability: <7 minutes.

pipeline:

Performance and Accuracy

Let's talk numbers. We've been running the GNN in production for 8 months. Here are the metrics that matter:

Sanctions address identification: On a held-out test set of 50,000 addresses (including 1,200 known sanctioned addresses), the GNN achieves 96.3% precision and 94.7% recall at identifying sanctioned addresses. The false positive rate is 0.8% — meaning 99.2% of non-sanctioned addresses are correctly classified as clean. This is a significant improvement over our previous tabular XGBoost model, which achieved 89.1% precision and 82.4% recall on the same dataset.

Mixer detection: On a manually-curated dataset of 8,500 mixer-related addresses, the GNN achieves 91.2% F1 score. The key advantage is in detecting unknown mixers — addresses not present in any training label. Here the GNN's inductive capability shines. It generalizes to new mixers by recognizing structural patterns, not memorized addresses.

Inference latency: For a single-address query (the common case for our Guard integration), embedding computation takes ~12ms on GPU, ~45ms on CPU fallback. For batch inference (500K nodes), the full pipeline completes in ~90 seconds. The 5-minute update interval means the on-chain risk data is never more than 5 minutes stale.

Training: We retrain the model monthly on the full graph using semi-supervised learning. The model is trained on labeled nodes (sanctions, exchanges, known bad actors) and uses the graph structure to propagate labels to unlabeled nodes. Training takes ~6 hours on 4x A100 GPUs.

Why This Matters for Compliance

The regulatory landscape for on-chain finance is hardening. The EU's MiCA framework requires "transaction monitoring systems capable of detecting suspicious patterns." Hong Kong's stablecoin licensing regime mandates "ongoing monitoring of customer transactions." The US Treasury has made clear that compliance programs must be "reasonably designed" to prevent sanctions violations — and "we didn't know" is not a defense.

A compliance system that only checks addresses against static lists is not "reasonably designed." It's a checkbox. Regulators know this. They're asking for behavioral analysis, pattern detection, relationship mapping. The GNN provides exactly this — and it does so with an audit trail that regulators can verify independently.

Every GNN inference result is logged: the input features, the graph neighborhood sampled, the model version, the output score, and the timestamp. This is not a black-box AI making opaque decisions. It's a transparent, reproducible risk assessment that happens to use a neural network for the computation.

More importantly, the GNN enables proactive compliance. Instead of reacting to sanctions list updates (which can lag real-world activity by days or weeks), the GNN identifies risky patterns before they appear on official lists. The address we flagged in January — two hops from a mixer, connected through a suspicious wallet creation pattern — was on the OFAC list three days later. The GNN didn't have a crystal ball. It had the graph.

For stablecoin issuers, DeFi protocols, and RWA platforms, this means a compliance layer that doesn't just keep you out of trouble — it keeps you ahead of trouble. And in an industry where regulatory action can be existential, being ahead is the only safe place to be.
